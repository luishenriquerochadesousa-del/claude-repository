07

How to pay for it, given you'll sell it

You told me you would not keep the car forever. That single constraint eliminates two of the three products a German dealer will offer you.

Leasing NO

Structurally attractive — fixed cost, hand it back, no residual risk. But you cannot sell a leased car, so the moment you want out you are stuck paying to the end of the term. Mileage caps bite, modifications are out, and used performance cars lease badly anyway.

Ballonfinanzierung NO

The dealer standard: low monthly, large final payment. Selling mid-term is messy, the balloon routinely exceeds the car's real value if you go over the contracted mileage, and you pay interest on the whole sum throughout. Built for people who never do the arithmetic.

Bank Ratenkredit YES

A plain instalment loan from a direct bank — ING, DKB, Santander. You own the car outright, so you can sell whenever you like and settle the balance. German consumer credit law caps early-repayment compensation at 1% (0.5% inside the final year), so exiting is cheap.

Cash NOT FOR YOU

No interest and total freedom, but it sinks €38,000 into a depreciating asset and costs you sixteen months of waiting. You said you would rather not burn through the money to enjoy the car — that instinct is correct here, and the equity table below shows why.

There is a second, quieter advantage to the bank loan: arriving as a cash buyer. A dealer quoting you a financed price is pricing the finance commission into the deal. Walking in with funds already approved is worth 3–8% off the screen price — on a €38,000 car that is €1,140 to €3,040, which is most of the interest you will pay.

How much deposit, and what it buys you

Here is the thing that makes financing work for you specifically. A used performance car depreciating 6% a year loses value far slower than a 48-month loan pays down. So you are in positive equity from the first year at every deposit level — which means you can sell whenever you want, settle the loan, and put the difference into whatever is next.

The lever nobody mentions

German car insurance is priced off your Schadenfreiheitsklasse — the count of consecutive claim-free years you personally have on a policy. You have never insured a car in Germany, which puts you at SF 0, paying roughly 100% of base premium. On an M4 in Munich that is somewhere around €4,000–4,600 a year.

Three or four claim-free years takes you to SF 3–4 and around 65–70% of base. Over a three-year hold that difference is worth €4,000 to €6,000 — comparable to the entire depreciation on the F82.

  • Start the clock now. Insure something cheap in your own name the moment you land in Munich. Every claim-free year counts whether the car is a Polo or an M4.
  • Bring your Portuguese history over. If you have ever held a policy in your own name in Portugal, request the declaração de não sinistralidade from that insurer. Most German insurers will credit EU no-claims history — this can skip you several classes at a stroke, and it costs one email.
  • Ask your parents about an SF transfer. German insurers allow a Schadenfreiheitsklasse to be transferred from a parent who is giving up a vehicle. If either of them has a long claim-free record and a car they are retiring, that record can move to you.
  • Saisonkennzeichen 03–10. Register the car for eight months instead of twelve. Insurance and Kfz-Steuer both drop by around a third, you skip a €1,400 set of winter tyres, and the car never sees road salt. In a city with Munich's public transport this costs you almost nothing.
  • Garage it, raise the excess, declare low mileage. A €1,000 comprehensive excess, 9,000 km declared, and a registered night-time garage space stack to another 25–35% off. Workshop binding saves more again if you can live without main-dealer stamps.

Do the first two this autumn. They cost nothing, they compound quietly in the background, and by the time you are actually buying they will have taken four figures a year off the running-cost line in section 06.

What could break this

Moving back to Portugal

The big one. Importing a €57,000 German car into Portugal triggers ISV, which on a 4.0 V8 with high CO₂ runs into the tens of thousands. There is an exemption for transferência de residência — you must have owned the car six months, lived abroad twelve, apply within twelve months of moving and keep it a further twelve. Miss the paperwork and the tax exceeds the car's value. Given you were job-hunting across Europe this summer, treat this as a live risk, not a footnote.

Munich rent overshooting

My €1,600 is a reasonable one-bedroom. Munich can easily be €1,900 warm for the same flat. Every €100 over budget pushes the purchase back about two months. A WG for the first year is worth roughly nine months off the timeline.

Another job change

You have moved employer once already this year, with a zero-income month in the gap. A €473/month loan payment through a second gap is survivable; the same gap with no buffer is not. This is why the emergency fund comes before the car and not alongside it.

Stretching the deposit down

The failure mode here isn't the car, it's taking 15% down to get in six months sooner and going €54/month backwards from day one. A bank will happily lend you more than you can run. Keep the deposit at 35% or above, and if the money isn't there in October 2027, wait the extra two months rather than shaving the deposit.

The honest opportunity cost. Three years of the F82 costs about €38,982 in depreciation and running. The same money left invested at 6% would be worth roughly €42,500 by the end. That is what the car costs you in real terms — and it is a completely reasonable thing to spend money on at 27, as long as you are choosing it rather than discovering it later.

Where the money should live

One principle settles both questions: the time horizon picks the asset, and the account is just a container. A separate portfolio is a behavioural tool — it stops you raiding one goal to fund another — but it does nothing for returns. What matters is what is inside it.

Which is why the car and the house get opposite answers.

The car: yes, separate — and out of equities

You need €13,300 in October 2027. That is twelve months away, and twelve-month money must not be in stocks. Not because equities are bad, but because a 20% drawdown in month eleven leaves you choosing between delaying the car six months and selling at a loss to buy a depreciating asset. That is a genuinely bad position and it is entirely avoidable.

You already own the right instrument. Certificados de Aforro Série F pays a reasonable rate, is capital-guaranteed, and is liquid after three months — it is a better home for the car deposit than anything in a brokerage account. A German Tagesgeld or a money-market ETF does the same job.

The house: no, not yet

Money for a seven-to-ten year goal has exactly the same optimal allocation as general long-term wealth: broad equities. A second account holding the same ETFs under a different label adds admin and no return. Worse, carving out a "house fund" now is a psychological commitment to a decision you have not made and should not make yet — which country.

Open it when the goal is real: two to three years out, city decided, and a number attached. At that point you start a glidepath, moving it out of equities in stages so you are not exposed on the month you need it. Until then it is simply your long-term portfolio, which you have already ring-fenced and which is doing its job.

Two free wins while you're restructuring

  • File a Freistellungsauftrag with Trade Republic. Germany gives you a €1,000 Sparerpauschbetrag each year; without the exemption order the broker withholds 25% Abgeltungsteuer plus Soli from the first euro of interest or dividends. It takes two minutes in the app and you are currently paying tax you do not owe.
  • Ask Helsing about vermögenswirksame Leistungen. Many German employers pay up to €40 a month into a designated savings plan on top of salary. At €80k you are above the income limit for the state Arbeitnehmersparzulage, but the employer's contribution is free money regardless — and it is one of the few genuine reasons to open a specific separate account.
  • Check the Portuguese Aforro against German tax. As a German resident your worldwide income is taxable here. Trade Republic withholds automatically; CTT does not, so that interest belongs on Anlage KAP. Worth one conversation with a Steuerberater in your first German tax year rather than a correction later.
