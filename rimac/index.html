Section 5Stories, retold for a CAE panel

The same stories as before, with the emphasis moved. For designers you led with the geometry and the drawing. For analysts, lead with the model, its assumptions and how you knew it was right.

Story 1 — Volvo multi-scale cell model (the main one)

My thesis at Volvo Cars was a multi-scale FE method to predict how lithium-ion cells respond in a crash, including strain-rate dependence. A cell is a layered structure: anode, cathode, separator, current collectors. Modelling every layer at full-cell scale is too expensive, and the full-cell models Volvo had needed a lot of parameters fitted to whole-cell tests. So I worked bottom-up. I ran layer-level tests myself to get the constituent behaviour, built a representative volume element of the layer stack, and homogenised it into an equivalent continuum. Then I calibrated the full-cell model against quasi-static and dynamic cell tests, using a DOE and optimisation loop in MATLAB. I did mesh convergence and energy-balance checks throughout. The result was 33% better accuracy against test, about 64% fewer parameters needing calibration, and calibration time halved.

Follow-ups to have cold

  • “Why multi-scale?” Fewer free parameters means fewer ways to get a good fit for the wrong reason. Physically grounded parameters transfer better to other cell formats and load cases.
  • “What strain-rate law?” Name it. If it was a Cowper–Symonds or Johnson–Cook style multiplier, write it: σd/σs = 1 + (ε̇/C)1/p. If it was tabulated curves per strain rate, say so.
  • “How did you validate, as opposed to calibrate?” The critical distinction. Be clear which tests were used for fitting and which were held out. If none were held out, say so, and say what you would hold out next time. They will respect that more than a vague answer.
  • “Where was it wrong?” Have one specific discrepancy and its likely cause, for example the post-peak or densification regime, or anisotropy in-plane versus through-thickness.
  • “What would you do differently?” Check whether the coarse model already answered the question before refining (your own self-critique from the Helsing prep). Hold out a validation test.

Story 2 — Tesla thermal study (simulation that changed a decision)

At Tesla the plan of record was to delete copper heat-sink blocks at a 100-amp interface to simplify the build. Nobody asked me to check it, but there was a G10 structural part right next to it. I'd never opened 3DEXPERIENCE, so I taught myself its thermal workflow and ran a steady-state screening model: a current-derived heat load at the contact, conduction through the copper, convection to still air. It showed the G10 heading towards its continuous service temperature under sustained running. The decision was reversed before freeze. That avoided about $7,500 of rework and weeks of schedule. It was a screening model, not a qualification model. It answered a yes/no question, and that was all it needed to do.

Say “continuous service temperature”, never “melting point”. G10 is a thermoset laminate and it does not melt. An FEA expert will notice.

Why it lands with this panel: it shows CAE driving a decision, the tool learned fast (your HyperWorks argument), and honesty about how far the model can be trusted.

Story 3 — TEKEVER shaker jig (prediction, then test)

I designed fixtures for vibration-testing antennas on TEKEVER's shaker. The governing requirement was that the fixture must not resonate inside the test band, otherwise you amplify the input and fail parts that are fine. Before opening CAD I estimated the first mode from stiffness and mass and targeted two to three times the top of the excitation band. Then I checked it in FE and validated it on the shaker. There was no fixture resonance in band, and the design was reused for other variants.

Have ready: how you measured the jig's response (sine sweep, accelerometer on the fixture) and how close the prediction was. If you don't remember the number, say “within the margin I'd designed for”. Do not invent a percentage.

Story 4 — Apple (complex test data → root cause)

At Apple I did failure analysis on iPhones already in customers' hands: teardowns, CT, materials analysis and reliability statistics including Monte Carlo. I traced a critical component failure affecting over a million users to its root cause. Separately, I characterised how 100+ field returns had actually failed, built a seal-leak test method from that, and it became a new module-level reliability test. The lesson I carry into CAE is that the test you didn't think of is the one that bites. A model only covers the failure modes you put into it.

Story 5 — the mistake (CAE-specific, use this one)

The clearest technical mistake I've made was trusting a boundary condition I'd assumed rather than checked. On the Tesla end effector I modelled an interface as better constrained than it really was once it was bolted up. The hardware didn't behave the way the model said, and it took me about a week, changing one variable at a time, to find it. Two things changed. When I present an analysis now I say explicitly which inputs are measured and which are assumed, so someone else can catch me. And I treat boundary conditions as the first suspect, not the last. In my experience they're wrong more often than the material model or the mesh.

This is a strong answer for an FEA panel because “the BCs were wrong” is the most common real-world FEA failure, and every one of them has lived it.

Story 6 — the second mistake (over-fidelity)

The other pattern is over-investing in fidelity. On the Volvo thesis I spent a long time refining model detail before checking whether the coarse version already answered the question. Now I ask what decision the analysis feeds, build the cheapest model that can make that decision, and refine only if the answer sits near the limit.

The universal set-up checklist (say this when asked “how would you set up X?”)

  1. Question: what decision does this feed, what is the target, what is the pass criterion?
  2. Hand estimate of the answer's order of magnitude (beam, plate, energy).
  3. Model scope: what is in, what is cut, how the cut boundary is represented (often the hardest choice).
  4. Mesh: element type (shell for thin CFRP and sheet, solid for castings and thick blocks), size from the feature and the gradient, quality criteria.
  5. Materials: source of the card, rate dependence, failure; for CFRP, the ply stack and orientation.
  6. Connections: hinges, bolts (beam plus preload or rigid spider), adhesive (cohesive or tied), welds.
  7. BCs and loads: avoid over-constraint; distribute point loads (RBE3 for loads, RBE2 only where truly rigid).
  8. Analysis type: linear / nonlinear / explicit, and why.
  9. Checks: reactions vs applied, units, mass, free-free modes for disconnected parts, energy (explicit), convergence (implicit), a mesh study at the hotspot.
  10. Report: answer, margin, assumptions (measured vs assumed), recommendation, and what would change the conclusion.

Section 8Implicit vs explicit — the answer you must give fluently

Almost certainly the first technical question, because it is in the first line of the posting. Practise it until it takes 90 seconds and needs no notes. Then be ready for the next three levels down.

Both solve the equation of motion, Mü + Cú + fint(u) = fext, but they march through time differently. Implicit methods, like Newmark or HHT for dynamics, or a pure static solve, find equilibrium at the end of each increment. You assemble a tangent stiffness matrix and iterate with Newton–Raphson until the residual converges. Each increment is expensive, but stable for large steps, so it suits static and slow problems: stiffness, sag, dent, durability load cases, modal. Explicit methods, central difference, use the equilibrium at the current time to get accelerations directly from a lumped mass matrix: ü = M−1(fext − fint). No matrix inversion, no iterations, so each step is cheap. But it's only conditionally stable: the time step has to stay below the time a stress wave needs to cross the smallest element, Δt ≤ Le/c, with c = √(E/ρ). That means microsecond steps, so it suits short, highly nonlinear events with a lot of contact: impact, crash, slam, pedestrian. Rule of thumb: implicit when the event is slow and the nonlinearity is mild; explicit when the event is fast, or when contact and failure would make Newton–Raphson fail to converge.

The three levels down

ProbeAnswer
Why is explicit conditionally stable?Central difference is stable only if Δt ≤ 2/ωmax, where ωmax is the highest natural frequency of the mesh. For one element, ωmax ≈ 2c/Le, which gives the Courant (CFL) limit Δt ≤ Le/c. Solvers apply a safety factor of about 0.9.
What controls the explicit time step in practice?The smallest, stiffest, lightest element. One bad element sets the step for the whole model. So mesh quality and minimum element size matter more in explicit, and you check the time-step report for the controlling elements.
What is mass scaling and what's the risk?Adding non-physical mass to small elements to raise their critical Δt (Δt ∝ √ρ). It's fine if added mass is a small fraction of the total, commonly kept below a few percent, and isn't concentrated where inertia matters. Too much and you change the dynamics: kinetic energy, impact force, wave speed. Always report the added mass.
Can you run a quasi-static problem in explicit?Yes, and it's common when contact or failure make implicit hard. Apply the load slowly enough that inertia is negligible: kinetic energy a small fraction (about <5–10%) of internal energy, smooth ramp, check the result doesn't change if you slow it further. Speeding up the load or scaling mass shortens run time but both add inertia, so check.
What energy checks do you do in explicit?Total energy roughly conserved (energy ratio near 1, no unexplained growth). Hourglass energy small compared with internal energy, a common target being under about 5–10%. Contact or sliding-interface energy small and not negative. Added mass reported. Kinetic and internal energy following the physics of the event.
What is hourglassing?Reduced-integration elements (one integration point) have zero-energy deformation modes: the element deforms in an hourglass pattern without the single point seeing any strain. Controlled with hourglass stiffness or viscosity, which adds artificial energy, so you monitor it. Fixes: finer mesh, avoid point loads, distribute contact, a better hourglass formulation, or fully integrated elements where it matters.
Why not always use fully integrated elements?Cost, plus locking. Fully integrated linear elements suffer shear locking in bending (too stiff) and volumetric locking for nearly incompressible materials (plasticity, rubber). Remedies include reduced integration with hourglass control, selective or reduced integration, incompatible-mode elements, or quadratic elements.
When does Newton–Raphson fail?Sudden contact changes, material softening, buckling or snap-through (the tangent stiffness becomes singular at a limit point), a bad initial guess, increments that are too big. Remedies: smaller increments, line search, contact stabilisation, arc-length (Riks) for limit points, or switch to explicit.
Implicit dynamic — when?When the response is dynamic but slow compared with wave propagation: low-frequency transients, door closing with soft contact, some drop cases. Large steps are allowed, but the time step still has to resolve the frequencies you care about (roughly 20 steps per period of the highest mode of interest). Numerical damping (HHT α) kills spurious high-frequency noise.
Implicit or explicit for door slam?Usually explicit for the transient, because it's a short contact event with seal and latch nonlinearity. Then stress histories go to fatigue. Some OEMs use implicit dynamic or a quasi-static equivalent for parts of it. Say you'd follow the method that is already correlated to their test.

Other fundamentals Ivan may test

  • Three sources of nonlinearity: material (plasticity, hyperelasticity, damage), geometric (large displacement or rotation, stress stiffening, buckling), boundary (contact, friction).
  • Shells vs solids: shells when thickness is much smaller than the other dimensions (a ratio around 1:10 or less). Mid-surface extraction, offsets, through-thickness integration points (at least 3 for plasticity, 5 is common). Use solids for castings, thick bosses and inserts.
  • Tets vs hexes: linear tets (CTETRA4/C3D4) are overly stiff, so use second-order tets (10-node) for implicit stress. Hexes are more efficient per DOF when the geometry allows.
  • Singularities: stress at a sharp re-entrant corner or a point load/constraint does not converge with mesh refinement. It keeps rising. A real stress peak converges. Read stress a few elements away, model the real fillet, or use a fatigue or hot-spot approach.
  • RBE2 vs RBE3: RBE2 is rigid, adds stiffness, and is right for a genuinely rigid part (bolt head on a thick boss). RBE3 is an interpolation element that distributes load without adding stiffness, right for applying a load or mass. Using RBE2 to apply loads on a thin panel artificially stiffens it.
  • Inertia relief: for unconstrained bodies (a full car in a manoeuvre), balance applied loads with inertial loads instead of fixing arbitrary points.
  • Contact: penalty (a spring, which allows small penetration; stiffness affects time step and convergence) vs Lagrange multiplier (exact, adds DOFs). Node-to-surface vs surface-to-surface. Initial penetrations must be cleaned. Self-contact in crash folding.
  • Modal: eigenvalue problem (K − ω²M)φ = 0. Lanczos for large sparse models. Free-free run: six rigid-body modes near 0 Hz, and a seventh near zero means a disconnected part, which is a great model check.
  • Linear buckling: eigenvalue on the stress stiffness. It is non-conservative for imperfection-sensitive shells, so follow with nonlinear analysis using imperfections seeded from the buckling modes.

Section 10Correlation & physical testing

The posting says “participate in physical testing to validate and refine simulation models”. That is your strongest card. You have done it yourself (Volvo, TEKEVER), not just received a test report.

The correlation loop (how to describe it)

  1. Plan the test with the model. Use pre-test simulation to place strain gauges and accelerometers at points that are informative and not in high gradients, and to pick load levels.
  2. Match the test, not the requirement. Model the real fixture, the real constraint stiffness, the real loading device and the measured thickness or ply count. Most correlation gaps come from boundary conditions.
  3. Compare like with like. Same filtering (e.g. CFC filters for crash data), same sampling point, same coordinate system.
  4. Metrics: stiffness (slope), peak force, displacement at peak, energy absorbed, strain at gauges, frequencies within a few percent and MAC > about 0.9 for mode shapes. For full fields, DIC (digital image correlation) against contour plots.
  5. Diagnose in order: BCs and fixture → geometry and thickness → connections → material card → mesh. Change one thing at a time. Never tune a material parameter to fix a boundary-condition error.
  6. Validate on a case you didn't calibrate on. Otherwise you have only shown that you can fit.
  7. Feed back: update the modelling guideline so the next model starts correlated.
The thing I'm strictest about is the difference between calibration and validation. In the thesis I calibrated against quasi-static and dynamic cell tests. The question I'd now always ask is which test is held out, because a model that only matches the tests it was fitted to hasn't been validated yet.

Only say the second half of that if it is consistent with what you actually did. If you did hold out a test, say which. That makes it a much stronger story.

Composite specifics for correlation

  • Scatter in CFRP properties is larger than in metals. Use B-basis or A-basis allowables for strength, and mean values for stiffness correlation.
  • Real ply drops, overlaps and fibre angles (from draping) differ from nominal. Draping simulation can feed fibre angles to the FE model.
  • Failure criteria (max stress or strain, Tsai–Wu, Hashin, Puck) predict onset differently. Matrix cracking and delamination need separate treatment (cohesive zones for delamination and adhesive joints).
  • Environmental knock-down: hot/wet conditions reduce matrix-dominated properties.
